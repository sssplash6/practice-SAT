## 2. Subtypes

### 2a. Main purpose (25 items: easy 9, medium 6, hard 10)

The student names what the whole text is doing: the rhetorical act (describe, explain, argue, portray, convey) plus a summary of the content at the level of the whole text. Every sentence must be accounted for; an option that fits only one sentence is wrong.

Two variants:
- **Informational purpose** (15: easy 6, medium 2, hard 7). Answers such as "To explain an effort made by the city of Portland to reduce demolition waste and some results of that effort" (1.8) or "It discusses the findings of a study that evaluated the effects of exposing a plant species to lunar soil conditions" (3.8). In 4 items the correct act is evaluative: "To argue that ..." (1.22, 2.18), "To assert that ..." (3.16), and "It discusses the study ... to illustrate why caution is needed ..." (3.4). These passages carry explicit evaluative language ("deserve as much fame", "busted the myth", "we should not overstate", "aren’t as distinct as they seem").
- **Literary purpose** (10: easy 3, medium 4, hard 3). The student names the feeling, trait or situation an excerpt conveys: "To portray Polly’s reaction to Fanny’s friends" (1.5), "To emphasize the uniformity of both the town and the people who live there" (2.6), "It presents an account of a misunderstanding" (3.3). Two are poems (3.15 and 3.19, both hard).

Stems: "Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?" (22) and three near variants (above).

### 2b. Overall structure (17 items: easy 3, medium 9, hard 5)

The student must describe the sequence of rhetorical moves in order, at an abstract level, with generic nouns ("a researcher", "a common view", "a recent finding"). Options are 2-move ("It X, then Y" / "It X and then Y") in 13 items and 3-move ("It X, Y, and then Z") in 4 items, all hard (3.6, 3.9, 3.11, 3.17). Three structure items are literary (2.8 and 2.21 poems, "The speaker ..."; 2.22 prose). Stem: "Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?" (16) and "the text’s overall structure" (1).

### 2c. Function of a part (24 items: easy 11, medium 9, hard 4)

The student names the job a marked span does relative to the rest of the text. Target types: underlined portion (8), underlined sentence (10), underlined question (1, 1.7), underlined lines of a poem (1, 1.23), underlined phrase (1, 2.4), two underlined portions (1, 2.23), "the first sentence" (2.3) and "the third sentence" (3.14). In both ordinal items the sentence is also underlined in print. Underlined spans run 5 to 34 words, median 13.

Three function families (hand-coded):
- **Local link** (8: easy 2, medium 4, hard 2): the answer names the span's relation to a neighbor. "It provides an answer to the question in the previous sentence" (1.18); "It sets up the character description presented in the sentences that follow" (2.13); "It qualifies an earlier description of Mr. Verloc" (2.4); "It provides context that clarifies the significance of the information that follows in the rest of the sentence" (1.2); "It presents a generalization that is exemplified by the discussion of ..." (3.14).
- **Content role in an argument** (10: easy 4, medium 4, hard 2): the span is an example, a term, a definition, a limitation, a method, a mechanism, reasons, or a figure of extent. "It introduces a scientific term that is used in the discussion that follows" (1.11); "It describes a limitation of some studies about the origin of turtles" (1.16); "It underscores the extent of the CEP’s impact ..." (3.12, the span is "—more than 7,000 in all—").
- **Literary effect** (6: easy 5, medium 1): the span is a detail or image whose job is to show a feeling or trait. "It emphasizes Shakti’s sense of belonging in the landscape" (1.1, span: trees extending branches "like welcoming arms"); "To emphasize the extent of individuals’ struggles to articulate thoughts on art" (2.23, two axe-image spans).

Pointer phrases in function options: "previous sentence" in 9 options (2 correct), "following sentence / sentences that follow" in 10 (3 correct), "rest of the sentence" in 2 (1 correct), "in the text / discussion that follows" in 10 (2 correct). A wrong pointer (previous vs following) is itself a distractor device.

## 4. The correct answer

**Justification.** The key is the only option that is true of every part it mentions and leaves no major part out:
- Purpose keys summarize the whole arc, often with a two-part object that mirrors the passage ("an effort ... and some results of that effort", 1.8; "describe the silversword alliance and explain how the plant family became so varied", 3.18).
- Structure keys name each move in order and get the relation between moves right ("a recent finding that challenges that view", 2.17; "a third explanation ... that the author regards as plausible", 3.11).
- Function keys name the span’s role and, when relevant, the direction of the link ("in the following sentence").

**Wording.** Keys paraphrase at an abstract level; they do not echo the passage more than distractors do. Share of an option’s content words that also occur in the passage: purpose 0.30 correct vs 0.32 wrong, structure 0.13 vs 0.11, function 0.20 vs 0.21. Structure options are the most abstract (only about one content word in eight comes from the passage). Keys are modest: 13 of 66 keys (20%) contain a hedge (some, possible, might, may, helps, certain, particular, partly) against 22 of 198 wrong options (11%); 2 keys (3%) contain an absolute (all, most, always, impossible, only, every, many, widely) against 17 wrong options (9%). Typical hedged keys: "presents a possible answer" (2.24), "might occur among vertebrates" (2.14), "helps explain Cruse’s position" (3.10).

**Length.** Mean words, correct vs wrong: easy 12.8 vs 13.4, medium 15.6 vs 15.6, hard 18.6 vs 20.0. The key is the uniquely shortest option in 20 of 66 items (easy 8, medium 7, hard 5) and the uniquely longest in only 8 (3, 4, 1). Distractors gain length by adding invented specifics.

## 5. Distractor recipes

198 wrong options, one primary label each.

| Archetype | All | Purpose | Structure | Function | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Off-text invention (INV) | 83 | 30 | 30 | 23 | Adds an act, comparison, cause, motive or event the text never mentions |
| Wrong relation or label (REL) | 22 | 1 | 1 | 20 | Misnames the span’s job or its link (defines a term, poses a hypothesis, contradicts, previous vs following) |
| Reversal (REV) | 21 | 7 | 6 | 8 | Says the opposite of the text: fact, direction of change, outcome or emotion |
| Word or image hook (LIT) | 20 | 12 | 2 | 6 | Recycles a vivid word or figure from the text into a false literal claim |
| Wrong stance or speech act (STANCE) | 19 | 8 | 7 | 4 | Makes a neutral text argue, urge, criticize, dismiss or concede, or flips support into challenge |
| Overreach (BROAD) | 13 | 8 | 3 | 2 | Extends scope or strength beyond the text (all, whole city, impossible, recurring theme) |
| Partial (PART) | 11 | 6 | 1 | 4 | True of one sentence or the neighbor of the span, not of the target |
| Misattribution (SWAP) | 8 | 3 | 0 | 5 | Right claim, wrong holder or target (who feels what; behavior vs mechanism) |
| Wrong order (ORDER) | 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 | Right kinds of moves in the reverse sequence |

Examples (paraphrased, or quoted under 15 words):
- **INV**: in 1.22 (tool use in dolphins and cockatoos) all three wrong options are about intelligence, which the passage never mentions. 1.8 D: "compare them to similar laws in other cities" (no other city appears).
- **REL**: 2.3 offers four labels for a definition sentence (defines a phenomenon / introduces a problem / makes a claim that is challenged / presents a hypothesis); only the first is true. 2.13 A says the span elaborates on the previous sentence when it sets up the following ones.
- **REV**: 1.1 A says Shakti "feels uncomfortable near the river" when the trees welcome her. 3.8 C calls germination "an unattainable goal" though every seed germinated.
- **LIT**: 1.1 D turns "greeted her as an old friend" into "long-term friendships". 2.23 B turns the axe image into "the physical effort involved in ... construction of large-scale works of art". 2.4 D picks up "private" from the last line ("a private opinion").
- **STANCE**: 1.6 C "To urge people to protect the murals of San Francisco from decay" for a neutral overview. 2.24 A: a theory "then discusses facts that weaken it" when the facts support it.
- **BROAD**: 3.18 A and D extend the silversword story to "all plants that grow on the Hawaiian Islands". 2.11 B: "impossible for these works to serve the purpose their authors intended".
- **PART**: 3.16 A, an overview of access in the republican period, covers only sentence 1. 1.14 A describes the next sentence (tribes on the map), not the underlined one.
- **SWAP**: 1.20 C and D give Dot’s exercise to the narrator or to Celestine. 3.1 D says insects and mammals "behave similarly" where the text says their brain mechanisms resemble each other.
- **ORDER**: 2.16 C puts a method’s advantages first and a historian’s criticism second, inverting problem-then-approach.

Patterns by subtype and register:
- Purpose distractors lean on invention and word hooks (LIT 12 of 75); literary purpose distractors almost always include one literalized figure (2.9 D: the "cloud in space" becomes physical distance; 3.15 D: the "secret fire" becomes a secret Dido keeps).
- Function distractors are the only place REL dominates (20 of 72): sibling labels drawn from a small inventory (defines a term, introduces a problem, presents a hypothesis, contradicts, elaborates on the previous, contrasts, provides an example).
- Literary items use LIT (12 of 63 wrong options) and SWAP (5) far more than informational ones (8 of 135 and 3).
- Verbs that are never correct anywhere in this bank: compare/contrast (11 options, 0 keys); question, criticize, dismiss, concede, caution, urge, encourage, justify, evaluate, claim, contend, speculate, predict, propose (together 20 options, 0 keys).

What concretely separates the tiers:
- **Easy**: short, simple sentences; modern or very clear literary excerpts; the key often restates a sentence’s obvious job ("It provides an answer to the question in the previous sentence"). Distractors fail on content you can check by scanning (1.22: a topic never mentioned; 1.13 D: "largely overlooked" for an artist "known worldwide"). Structure distractors are entirely different templates.
- **Medium**: longer sentences, older literary prose (Dickens, Conrad, Eliot, Woolf), structure becomes the main task, and function items move from content to relation labels (REL 11 of 27 function distractors in medium). More than a third of the structure distractors keep the first move and break the second (10 of 27).
- **Hard**: denser academic prose (hegemonic, perihelion, intersubjectivity), three-move structures where distractors keep moves 1 and 2 and break move 3 (11 of 15 hard structure distractors are wrong only in a later move: 3.11, 3.17), purpose items whose wrong options are near misses in scope or emphasis (3.2 B "resist" vs the text’s "balanced"; 3.19 B a contrast of everyday life and serenity that is only partly there). The key often states a dual purpose ("discusses the study ... to illustrate why caution is needed", 3.4) or a precise relation ("a synopsis ... that illustrates a literary concept discussed in the following sentence", 3.5).

## 8. Generation blueprint

The common core: build every item from an explicit **move plan** (an ordered list of labeled moves with stance), have an author or language model write a passage that realizes exactly those moves on an original topic, and derive the key and the distractors from the plan by controlled edits. Structure and function answers then become nearly programmatic; purpose answers need one authored summary.

### 8a. Overall structure

- **Slots**: domain; entity type (researcher, artist, work, phenomenon, policy); template from 7b (2-move for easy and medium, 3-move for hard); per move: verb, abstract object, stance (neutral, supports, challenges, qualifies).
- **Answer logic**: "It [verb1] [object1], then [verb2] [object2 pointing back with 'that']". Keys use neutral verbs (describe, present, introduce, provide, discuss, detail, pose, address) and generic nouns; keep content-word overlap with the passage under about 0.2.
- **Distractors (programmatic edits of the plan)**:
  1. Wholly wrong template: sample another template that shares the domain’s nouns (method vs alternative method, debate, gap and strategy, contradictory conclusions). Use 2 of these in easy, 1 in medium, 0 to 1 in hard.
  2. Later-move break: keep move 1 (and 2 in hard), replace the last move with a stance flip (supports becomes weakens or casts doubt), an invented move (hypothesis, further research, comparison, resolution), or an overreach (impossible, altogether, only temporary).
  3. Occasional first-move break or order flip (at most 1 item in 10).
- **Programmatic**: template catalog, verb and noun lexicon, connectors, all distractor text.
- **Authored**: the passage (language model), constrained to contain each move as one or two sentences and no comparison, debate, method contrast or hypothesis that a distractor names.
- **Checks**: options parallel ("It ..." or "The text ..."), 2 or 3 moves joined by "then" or "and then"; length ratio longest/shortest under 1.6; key not uniquely longest; a labeling model maps each passage sentence to a move ID and must reproduce the plan; a solver model, without the key, must pick the key on 3 of 3 runs; an audit prompt must give a sentence-level reason why each distractor is false.
- **Risks**: the passage drifts and makes a distractor partly true (the most common failure); a key that is so generic it fits any passage; hard 3-move items where move 2 is ambiguous between "explains" and "evaluates".

### 8b. Main purpose

- **Slots, informational**: text type (study report, profile, effort and result, view then challenge, argument); the act (describe, explain, present, summarize, give an example, argue or assert only if the passage contains evaluative language); one-clause summary covering all moves.
- **Slots, literary**: character and situation (attribution and context line), the feeling or trait shown, one figurative image, the act (portray, convey, emphasize, establish).
- **Answer logic**: "To [act] [whole-text summary]" (or "It [acts] ..."). Two-part objects for two-move texts ("an effort ... and some results").
- **Distractors**: one INV (an absent comparison, motive, influence or change over time), one STANCE (urge, argue, criticize for a neutral text; neutral description for an argued one), and one of PART (one sentence’s content), BROAD (extend to all, whole city, many composers), LIT (literalize the figure, reuse a vivid word) or REV (opposite feeling or outcome). In hard items prefer BROAD, PART and REV near misses; in literary items always include one LIT.
- **Programmatic**: the act verb and its stance class, option frame, length balancing, the STANCE distractor (swap the act verb for one from the evaluative list and keep the topic).
- **Authored**: passage and summary; INV and LIT distractors need a writer who knows what is absent from the text.
- **Literary source rule**: do not invent attribution lines for fictional authors. Either use real public-domain excerpts (pre-1929, not used in the official bank) with an accurate "The following text is adapted from ..." line, or frame original prose without a fake citation.
- **Checks**: the act verb of the key is evaluative only when the passage contains an evaluative marker (should, deserve, must, we should not, likely correct); compare or contrast in the key only if the passage compares; solver and audit as above.
- **Risks**: purpose vs main idea confusion (keys that restate the claim without the act verb); stance ambiguity; literary items where more than one feeling is defensible.

### 8c. Function of an underlined part

- **Slots**: the move plan; the target span (sentence, clause or phrase, 5 to 35 words); its role from 7d; its neighbor (previous, following, rest of sentence) for local-link roles.
- **Answer logic**: "It [role verb] [role object] [pointer]". Role and pointer come straight from the plan.
- **Distractors**: two REL siblings that are definitely false for the span (from: defines a term, introduces a problem, presents a hypothesis, makes a claim that is challenged, contradicts, elaborates on the previous, sets up the following, provides an example); or the right role with the wrong pointer; plus one PART (the content of the neighboring sentence) or one INV. For literary spans: REV (opposite feeling), LIT (literal reading of the image), SWAP (feeling given to another character).
- **Programmatic**: `<u>` markup, stem choice by span type (portion, sentence, phrase, question, lines, portions, or "the first/third sentence"), REL and pointer distractors, position checks.
- **Authored**: the passage; PART, INV and literary distractors.
- **Checks**: exactly the planned span(s) marked, plural stem when two spans; pointer validity (no "previous sentence" for sentence 1, no "following sentence" for the last); ordinal stems match the sentence index; the span alone must not contain words that let a distractor be read as true.
- **Risks**: a span with two jobs (an example that also defines), so two labels are defensible; spans that are too short to have a function beyond decoration.

### 8d. Plan

- **Templates**: about 12 two-move and 4 three-move structure templates; 6 purpose frames (study report, profile, effort and results, view then challenge, argument, literary reaction or mood); 8 function roles (example, term or definition, limitation, method or mechanism, answer to question, set-up, qualification, literary emphasis). About 30 templates in all.
- **Items**: first batch of 120 (40 per tier) mirroring the bank’s mix: easy about 18 function, 15 purpose, 7 structure; medium about 15 structure, 13 function, 12 purpose; hard about 20 purpose, 12 structure (8 three-move), 8 function. Keep literary near 35% easy, 40% medium, 15 to 20% hard. Balance answer letters to 10 per letter per tier.
- **Build order**: (1) overall structure, because key and distractors fall directly out of the move plan and the edit rules are well defined; (2) function of an underlined part, whose REL and pointer distractors are programmatic; (3) informational purpose; (4) literary purpose and literary-effect function items last, since they need sourced public-domain excerpts and the most judgment in writing LIT and REV distractors.
