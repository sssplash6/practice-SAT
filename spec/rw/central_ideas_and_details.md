### A. Main idea (36)
Definition: pick the statement that the passage as a whole supports and that captures what it is mainly saying. Stems: the six main-idea wordings above, almost always "Which choice best states the main idea of the text?".

What the student must do: find the passage's governing claim (in informational texts usually a researcher's finding, a revised view, or a generalization about a person's work), then reject options that are false, unsupported, or stretch the claim. In literary texts, name the character's or speaker's state or situation in plain terms.

The surprise in this bank: the wrong answers to main-idea items are almost never true but minor details. Only 5 of 114 distractors in the main-idea family (4%) are true-but-narrow and 6 (5%) are too broad. The rest are false or unsupported (see section 5). So in this bank the main-idea task is mostly an accuracy test with a gist requirement, not a scope test.

Where the main idea sits (27 informational main-idea items): early, in sentence 1 or 2 (4: 1.18, 2.6, 2.7, 2.9); in a middle sentence (5: 1.11, 2.15, 2.18, 2.25, 3.11); in the last sentence (8: 1.8, 1.10, 1.12, 2.8, 2.12, 3.9, 3.13, 3.20); spread across two or more non-adjacent parts, so the key has to join them (10: 1.3, 1.7, 2.2, 2.5, 2.22, 3.4, 3.6, 3.12, 3.16, 3.21). The spread type dominates hard: 5 of the 9 informational hard main-idea items.

Literary main idea (9: 1.1, 1.14, 1.16, 2.4, 2.11, 2.13, 2.17, 3.14, 3.17): the key names the character or speaker and their state or situation ("The narrator remembers a trip he took and admits to getting lost."). For poems it can restate the poem's claim as a generalization (3.17: a calm port at evening can make a person appreciate old age).

### B. Central question (1: 2.20)
"Which question does the text most directly attempt to answer?" The passage asks its own question ("What explains its rise in popularity?") and answers it. All four options are questions about the same topic. The key restates the passage's own question. The three distractors ask things the text never takes up (how many other plural forms, first recorded use, use outside the US).

### C. Explicit detail (16)
Definition: the answer is stated in one sentence (sometimes two adjacent sentences) of the passage. Stems: "According to the text, ..." (13), "What does the text indicate about X?" (2), "The text makes which point about X?" (1). All easy and medium; none in hard.

What the student must do: find the sentence the stem points to (the stem always names the entity, often with a passage phrase such as "to examine the glyphs" or "galactic bone G47"), then match a paraphrase. Literary explicit-detail items (1.6, 1.17, 2.10) ask why a character does something or what is true of them.

### D. Supported detail (13)
Definition: the answer is not stated anywhere but follows from combining two statements, or from reading a figure of speech. Stems: "Based on the text, ..." (8, often with "most likely"), "What does the text most strongly suggest about X?" (4), "Which statement about X is best supported by information in the text?" (1), and 2.1 "what can be concluded about".

What the student must do: hold two premises and draw the conclusion the author leaves implicit. Examples of the logic:
- 2.19: the fossils are 55 million years old and the comparison is with the largest penguin "that exists today", so the species are extinct.
- 3.5: stressed corals turn white, and bleached corals then produce bright colors, so bright corals have been stressed.
- 3.18: fynbos roots spread to absorb nutrients, and seedlings near the roots grow five times slower, so the roots starve forest plants of nutrients.
- 3.19: historians of capitalism rely on archival data that does not exist for precolonial Africa, so the Africanists must be using other evidence.

Literary versions: 1.15 (a character's feeling, from "glanced indifferently" plus "regarded ... as an insult"), 3.2 (how townspeople regard a doctor, from the hat metaphor), 2.24 (what the simile says about the mind), 3.3 (the human "challenge" in a poem's lion/man analogy).

### E. Main purpose (1: 3.15)
A purpose question about a short poem praising Paul Laurence Dunbar. Options are "To ..." infinitive fragments. It belongs to Text Structure and Purpose.

### Literary attribution line
Every literary item opens with "The following text is from ..." (6) or "The following text is adapted from ..." (10), or once "The following text is [author]'s circa 1920 poem ...". Pattern: author's full name, possessive, year, genre word (novel, story, short story, poem), title. 10 of 17 then add one or two context sentences: who the characters are (1.6 "Radha Aunty is the narrator's aunt."), the situation (2.11, the narrator is in a hot-air balloon), or glosses of hard words (3.17 defines spars, moorings, riding-light). Editorial brackets replace archaic or missing words: [visits], [Bill], [stay], [all], [hurry], [Begin] (1.17, 2.10, 2.13, 3.14).

Contrast markers (However, But, Yet, Although, Surprisingly, rather than, instead) appear in 5 easy, 9 medium and 9 hard passages. The hard tier shifts from "what researchers found" to "what scholars used to think and why it is wrong or incomplete" (3.4 cerebellum, 3.8 disco, 3.19 capitalism in Africa, 3.21 antigravity).

Typical study-report passage (1.8, honeybees): S1 defines the behavior and who observed it; S2 describes it; S3 says when it is triggered and what it does; S4 gives the researchers' hypothesis with its evidence ("as it is not observed when other, larger predators approach"). The main idea is S4.

### Names, dates, places
- Real people and real studies throughout, named by role plus full name: "Ornithologist Sahas Barve", "archaeologist Veronica Waweru", "Ecologist Jessica Reichert and her team". Teams: "and her team" or "and team" (easy 3, medium 2), "and colleagues" (1, 5, 1), "et al." only in hard (3: 3.7, 3.9, 3.21).
- Strongly international: Kenya, Japan, Canada, Mexico, Ecuador, Peru, Poland, South Africa, the Himalaya, Indonesia, Turkey, Sri Lanka, Alabama. Many named subjects are women or people of color (Catlett, Izquierdo, Farmer, Moejoh, Taylor, Favela).
- Years appear in 13 informational passages (recent research years 2017 to 2023; historical spans such as 1025 to 1031 CE, 1791, 1934). Numbers are rarely central; exceptions are 2.27 (82.9% vs 72.7%) and 2.25 ("by 24 to 57 percent").
- Italic titles and species names are normal (In the Path of the Four Seasons, Mollisonia symmetrica).

### Vocabulary
Easy informational passages use everyday words with one or two field terms that are glossed in place ("glyphs, or drawings"; "Microplastics are pieces of plastic that are smaller than a grain of rice"). Medium adds unglossed academic words (displaced, conjecture, obscurity). Hard is dense academic prose with abstract nouns and Latinate verbs: "highly mediated", "premised on the rejection of overt artifice", "negative disconfirmation", "agential interests", "obviated", "longitudinal economic data". Hard options are just as dense ("gained currency", "unmindful of the extent to which"). Literary passages use period diction (doth, beginneth, e'en, proffer), and the hardest ones are poems built on an extended metaphor.

## 4. The correct answer

How it is justified:
- Main idea: the key is the one option that is fully supported and covers the passage's governing claim. In hard items it usually has to carry both halves of a contrast (3.16: the picturesque rejected artifice, yet art shaped judgments of it).
- Explicit detail: the key paraphrases one sentence. 1.19's key is nearly verbatim ("Some of the newer pits were carved on top of the older pits"); 2.23 turns "based on their ear and head movements" into "They watched how each cat moved its ears and head."
- Supported detail: the key states the unstated conclusion of two premises (section 2D) and usually carries "likely" or "most likely" to match the stem.

How it is worded:
- Paraphrase level rises with difficulty. Share of an option's content words that also appear in the passage: key vs distractor mean is 55% vs 44% (easy), 41% vs 31% (medium), 32% vs 38% (hard). By subtype: explicit detail 59% vs 35% (keys echo), supported detail 25% vs 32% (keys abstract, distractors borrow passage words), main idea 41% vs 40% (no signal). The key has the highest overlap of the four options in only 20 of 67 items, close to chance.
- Abstraction is the house style. Keys replace the passage's specifics with a category noun: dock roots and clay become "local resources" (2.6), a handheld X-ray analyzer becomes "a special tool" (2.12), a study of one species becomes "Fossil evidence can contribute..." (1.18), named characters become "A person ... someone he deeply admired" (2.17).
- Length gives nothing away. Key vs distractor mean length: 13.2 vs 12.6 words (easy), 15.3 vs 15.5 (medium), 19.8 vs 20.3 (hard). The key is the longest option in 23 items and the shortest in 22 (ties counted). Main-idea keys grow with tier: mean 15.7, 17.9 and 23.4 words.
- Hedging follows the passage's certainty (section 7). Superlatives are allowed when the text supports them: "the most definitive evidence to date" (2.22), "Tron features the most original music from her" (3.13).

## 5. Distractor recipes

Every one of the 201 wrong options was given one primary label.

| Archetype | Count | % | easy | med | hard | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plausible but unsupported | 78 | 39% | 18 | 38 | 22 | A sensible claim in the same domain that the text never addresses |
| Twisted detail | 39 | 19% | 14 | 12 | 13 | Reuses passage nouns but changes the relation (who, cause, direction, mechanism) |
| Contradicts the text | 37 | 18% | 8 | 16 | 13 | Negates or reverses a stated point |
| Literal or tonal misreading | 13 | 6% | 3 | 5 | 5 | Takes a figure literally or misreads a character's feeling (literary only) |
| Overstated | 11 | 5% | 6 | 3 | 2 | Pushes the text to first, most, main, favorite, both |
| Invented comparison | 10 | 5% | 0 | 3 | 7 | Ranks or compares against something the text never compares |
| True but narrow / true but not the answer | 7 | 3% | 5 | 2 | 0 | A real passage detail that does not answer the question |
| Too broad | 6 | 3% | 3 | 2 | 1 | Generalizes past the text's scope |

The main-idea family (114 distractors) and the detail family (87) use nearly the same mix. Main-idea distractors are more often twisted (24 vs 15) and more often too broad or too narrow (11 vs 2). In the 51 literary distractors, literal or tonal misreading is 25% (13), the second biggest group after unsupported claims (17).

Recipes with examples:
- **Plausible but unsupported.** Invent a motive, a later event or an evaluation that fits the topic. In 2.27 the text gives a study's numbers and rules out one explanation; distractor C says the gap "could be due to inaccuracies in the survey results". In 1.6 the narrator looks at photos to recall his aunt; a distractor says he wants inspiration for a story. In 2.20 all three distractors are on-topic questions the passage never asks.
- **Twisted detail.** Keep the passage's vocabulary and change one link. 1.4 recombines medium and subject: "A painting of a piñata", "A sculpture of Jesse Valadez". 2.18 A reverses the direction of influence (Izapa copied the small settlements, instead of the reverse). 3.10 D gives a wrong mechanism: the beads absorb heat from the particles, when the text says the light focuses on the beads instead.
- **Contradicts the text.** Negate the finding, or swap in the expectation the text rejects. 2.16 C reports the expected uniform field as the result. 2.10 A gives the narrator's first guess, which the text then drops ("at last we began to see..."). 2.2 A ends "contradicting previous predictions" when the study supports them.
- **Literal or tonal misreading.** 3.3 A turns the zoo-cage image into humans failing to "tame certain wild animals"; 3.14 B reads "a journey in my head" as planning a trip; 1.1 C reads Heidi's scream of delight as fright; 2.13 A turns regret into celebration.
- **Overstated.** 3.11 D turns the text's "one or both of the studies" into "both"; 1.7 A says the researchers "were the first to confirm"; 1.17 B and D invent a "favorite".
- **Invented comparison.** Mostly hard. 3.7 B and C compare conventional fabrication with "other perovskite solar cell architectures" and "other established approaches", neither discussed; 3.5 B and D compare bright corals with white or brown corals on things the text never ranks.
- **True but narrow.** 1.19 B: Waweru did notice rows of pits, but that is not why she thinks the game was played for a long time. 1.10 C restates the definition sentence as the main idea.
- **Too broad.** 1.8 D extends honeybee shimmering to "Several different species of insects"; 2.25 B says social media "is revolutionizing" the fashion industry.

Structural habits:
- Parallel frames. In 27 items all four options start with the same word (main idea 7 of 36, explicit detail 10 of 16, supported detail 9 of 13). The options then differ only in one slot, which forces a real reading. In 2.2 each option ends with a verdict on a prior prediction ("lending new support", "contradicting", "complicating", "challenging"); only the key's verdict is right. In 3.3 every option has the form "They ... because of [cause]".
- Hedges are parallel too. In 3.19 all four options say "They likely ...". Across the bank "likely" appears in 13 distractors and only 4 keys.
- The three distractors in an item usually mix archetypes. Only 5 items use one archetype for all three: 2.6, 2.20, 2.21 and 2.27 (three unsupported claims) and 1.4 (three noun recombinations).

In practice:
- **Easy**: short plain sentences, one clearly stated finding or feeling ("With glowing eyes Heidi devoured the scene"), questions answered by locating one sentence, keys that echo it, distractors that are easy to rule out (word swaps, invented favorites, contradictions).
- **Medium**: main idea dominates (15 of 27); the key must join two sentences or abstract the examples into a category; distractors share the key's frame and differ in one slot (2.2); first poems appear.
- **Hard**: no "According to" items; nearly half are one-step inferences; passages revise an earlier view or describe a mechanism in academic language; the main-idea key has to carry a concession and the passage's exact hedge (3.21: the earlier theorists were justified then, but the rationale no longer holds); distractors borrow more passage words than the key and include invented comparisons that sound technical; poems rely on extended metaphor (Cullen's cages, Shakespeare's journey).

Scope:

- Abstracts the subject or examples into a category: 9 (1.18, 2.5, 2.6, 2.9, 2.12, 2.17, 2.25, 3.11, 3.17). 3.11 keeps the example as "Wild animals such as the Western scrub-jay".
- Two-part keys that hold a contrast, concession or cause: 10 (1.14, 2.2, 2.7, 2.13, 3.4, 3.9, 3.11, 3.14, 3.16, 3.21), 6 of them hard.

Hedging (16 of 36 keys carry at least one hedge; 15 of the 27 informational keys, but only 1 of the 9 literary keys):
- Modal verbs can / could / may / might: 8 (1.7, 1.11, 1.18, 2.15, 2.25, 3.9, 3.11, 3.17).
- Belief or attribution verbs think / predict / suggest / inferred / has been thought: 6 (1.8, 1.10, 2.18, 3.4, 3.9, 3.21).
- Adverbs and quantifiers likely / often / in part / primarily / one potential / except in certain circumstances: 5 (2.9, 2.12, 2.18, 3.4, 3.20).

The hedge copies the passage's own certainty: "Researchers hypothesize" becomes "Researchers think" (1.8); "may be storing" becomes "Ecologists predict" (1.10); "proposed that this wobble could be due to" becomes "could be explained by" (1.7). Keys with no hedge come from passages that state facts flatly (2.6, 3.13). Across all 67 items, distractors use "first" 5 times (keys never), "most" 9 times (keys twice, both supported), "likely" 13 times (keys 4). A hedge alone does not mark the key: on a broader word-list count (which also catches some, one, sometimes), main-idea keys contain a hedge word 47% of the time (17 of 36) and main-idea-family distractors 25% (27 of 108 in the 36 main-idea items).

## 8. Generation blueprint

### Template families

**T1. Main idea, informational study report** (the largest group; easy to hard).
- Slots: field; researcher name, role and team phrase (by tier: "and her team", "and colleagues", "et al."); object of study; method; finding; finding's certainty (fact, found, suggests, hypothesize, may); implication or next step; optional gloss of a term.
- Passage: 3 to 5 sentences, 70 to 100 words, in the order topic -> who and how -> finding -> implication. The governing claim goes in the last sentence (easy) or is split between finding and implication (medium).
- Key: [source frame] + finding at the passage's certainty, with examples abstracted into a category.
- Distractors (pick 3 different kinds): an unsupported domain extension; a twisted detail (same nouns, reversed cause or direction); a contradiction (negate the finding, or "contradicting earlier predictions" when it supports them); at medium, one overstated option (first, confirmed, most).

**T2. Main idea, view then correction** (medium and hard).
- Slots: holder of the earlier view; the view; the turn word (However, But, Yet); new evidence or argument; optional reason for the gap.
- Key: a two-part sentence, "Although/While [earlier view], [revision]", or "[subject] has been thought to X, but Y".
- Distractors: affirms the earlier view (contradiction); revises the wrong thing (twisted: the function of the pathway instead of the cerebellum, as in 3.4); an invented cause for the old view; an unsupported claim about later reception.

**T3. Main idea, profile** (easy and medium).
- Slots: person or organization; aim; one or two concrete examples; effect.
- Key: generalizes the examples ("draws on local resources"; "often depicts subjects using an unrealistic style").
- Distractors: one example presented as the purpose (twisted); an invented reputation ("widely acclaimed"); a chronological story the text does not tell ("has become increasingly abstract over time").

**T4. Main idea, literary prose and poem.**
- Slots: attribution line (author, year, genre, title); 0 to 2 context sentences; a 55 to 115 word excerpt (poem 45 to 90 words, 7 to 12 lines) with a clear emotional state or situation.
- Key: "[Character/The speaker] [state] [about object] (although/instead of ...)".
- Distractors: the opposite feeling; a misread signal (a scream of joy read as fear); an invented backstory or motive; for poems, the figure taken literally.

**T5. Explicit detail** (easy and medium).
- Slots: target sentence; stem frame from 7a (why, one reason, what challenge, how did ... determine, what was surprising, what is a difference); entity phrase copied from the passage.
- Key: paraphrase of the target sentence (synonyms, voice change), 45 to 65% content-word overlap.
- Distractors: one true passage detail that does not answer the stem; one recombination of passage nouns; one plausible outside reason. For "what was surprising" or "why does X", add the expectation or first guess the text rejects.

**T6. Supported detail** (medium and hard).
- Slots: premise P1 and premise P2 (in different sentences); the unstated conclusion C; the target entity in C.
- Stem: "What does the text most strongly suggest about X?", "Based on the text, what ... most likely ...?", or "Which statement about X is best supported ...?".
- Key: C, hedged with "likely" when the stem says "most likely"; all options take the same hedge.
- Distractors: an invented comparison with an entity named in the text; the conclusion with a wrong mechanism; a contradiction of P1 or P2; one unsupported claim that sounds technical.

**T7. Central question** (cheap, medium).
- The passage contains an explicit question and its answer. The key restates that question; the three distractors are on-topic questions the text never addresses (first recorded use, how many, where else).

### What can be programmatic and what needs a writer
- Programmatic: stem selection from the 7a tables; attribution-line formatting; team-phrase and naming conventions by tier; answer-letter balancing; option-length balancing; parallel first word for detail options; hedge propagation (if the stem says "most likely", add "likely" to all options); the T7 distractor questions from a small list of question frames.
- Writer or language model: every passage, every key paraphrase, every distractor sentence. Distractors cannot be produced by templates alone, because the twisted and invented-comparison kinds need knowledge of the passage's structure. The right job is "write a false statement of type K that reuses these passage nouns".

### Automatic checks that would catch broken items
1. Entailment pass: a judge model labels every option against the passage as entailed, contradicted or unsupported. Exactly one option (the key) may be entailed. For T6, the key must be entailed by two sentences together and not by any single sentence.
2. Gist pass (main idea): the judge confirms the key covers the governing claim, and no distractor is also a fair statement of the gist.
3. Hedge match: if the passage uses may, might, could, suggest, hypothesize or propose about the finding, the key must hedge too. "first", "only", "always", "never" and "most" are banned in the key unless the passage uses the same word.
4. Cue leakage: the key must not be the unique longest option more than about 35% of the time across a batch. Key overlap with the passage per tier should land near 55% (easy), 40% (medium) and 30% (hard), and in medium and hard at least one distractor should share more passage words than the key.
5. Shape: passage body 45 to 115 words (poems 40 to 95); 2 to 6 sentences; words per sentence near the tier band (about 19, 25, 28); options about 13, 15 and 20 words by tier; exactly four options; three distractors using at least two archetypes.
6. Style: curly quotes, no table or figure, and our UI rule against em dashes (the official bank uses them freely in passages, so write parentheticals with commas or parentheses instead).

### Quality risks
- **Factual risk.** The bank cites real researchers and real findings. Original items about real studies can misstate them. Either use invented researchers and invented but plausible findings (and say so in an internal note), or write passages only from facts we have checked.
- **Literary sourcing.** Excerpts must be public domain (pre-1929) or written fresh. Do not reuse the authors and excerpts in this bank. Period diction and an extended figure are what make hard poems hard, and a weak writer produces bland verse.
- **Second defensible answer.** Too-broad and twisted distractors can turn out true. The entailment pass is mandatory.
- **Over-hedged keys.** If keys always carry "can" or "may" and distractors never do, students learn the cue. Put hedges in some distractors (as the bank does) and leave some keys flat.
- **Hard items made hard only by vocabulary.** The bank's hard items are hard because of logic: a concession in the key, an inference across two sentences, invented comparisons. Hard words without those are not enough.

### Recommended scope
- 7 template families (T1 to T7), with T4 split into prose and poem, so 8 working templates.
- Target mix per tier, mirroring the bank: easy 55% main idea, 40% explicit detail, 5% supported detail; medium 55% main idea, 30% explicit detail, 10% supported detail, 5% central question; hard 55% main idea, 45% supported detail, no explicit detail. About 30% of each tier literary, with poems only from medium up.
- A realistic first bank: 40 easy (22 main idea, 16 explicit detail, 2 supported detail), 55 medium (30 main idea, 16 explicit detail, 6 supported detail, 3 central question), 45 hard (25 main idea, 20 supported detail), about 140 items.
- Build order: T1 (study-report main idea; the biggest group and the easiest to check) -> T5 (explicit detail, whose one-sentence evidence makes automatic checks reliable) -> T6 (supported detail, needed for hard) -> T2 (view-then-correction main idea for hard) -> T4 -> T3 -> T7.
