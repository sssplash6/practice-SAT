Stem: one wording, 75 of 75: "Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?" No figures, tables, notes, poems or underlined portions.

Options: always four connectives, each ending in a comma. 300 option slots use 74 distinct expressions (27 of them appear only once). An option is 1 to 4 words (mean about 1.6). Options are capitalized when the blank opens a sentence (61 items) and lowercase when it sits inside a sentence (14 items: 2 easy, 5 medium, 7 hard). Every option is a conjunctive adverb or adverbial phrase (however, as a result, that said...). The bank never offers coordinating or subordinating conjunctions (but, so, although, because), so all four options fit the grammar and only the logic decides.

## 2. Subtypes

The skill has a single task: choose the connective whose logical relation matches the link between what comes before the blank (A) and the clause after it (B). Items differ along two axes: the relation the key expresses, and where the blank sits in the sentence.

### 2a. By the relation of the key

| Subtype | Definition | Easy | Med | Hard | Total | Keys used |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Contrast | B opposes A: two subjects with opposite values, an old view against a new finding, appearance against reality | 8 | 5 | 2 | 15 | however 6, by contrast 5, though 2, by comparison 1, in contrast 1 |
| Concession / qualification | The writer grants a point that limits or seems to cut against the main claim without overturning it, or a claim holds despite an expectation against it | 0 | 0 | 6 | 6 | that said 2, of course 2, granted 1, nonetheless 1 |

| Addition / similarity | B is a further item of the same kind (another method, harm, act or theme), or a parallel case | 2 | 3 | 1 | 6 | in addition 2, additionally, moreover, what's more, likewise |
| Example | B is one concrete instance of the general claim A | 7 | 0 | 0 | 7 | for example 6, for instance 1 |
| Specification, restatement, emphasis | B narrows A to its exact content (specifically, in many cases), rewords A (that is, in other words), or adds a stronger fact in the same direction (in fact, indeed) | 2 | 3 | 5 | 10 | specifically 3, in many cases, that is 2, in other words, in fact 2, indeed |
| Sequence, time, culmination | B is the next step of a process, a later or earlier event, the end of a long effort, or what finally matters behind a surface fact | 8 | 2 | 2 | 12 | next 2, then, later, earlier, afterward, eventually, finally 2, previously, ultimately 2 |
| Other | an alternative method (alternatively, 3.16), a place just named (there, 2.8) | 0 | 1 | 1 | 2 | alternatively, there |

What the student must do in each case is the same: state A and B in a few words, name the relation, and reject every option whose relation is false. What changes is how the relation is signalled:

- Contrast: look for a second subject and an opposite value, or a turn from what was believed to what was found. The reasoning is "A says X, B says not-X".
- Concession: see that B (or the sentence after it) concedes or qualifies without cancelling A; often a "but" or "Still" after the blank carries the real turn.
- Cause / effect and purpose: check that A produces, motivates or justifies B.
- Addition / similarity: B is independent of A but points the same way.
- Example / specification / restatement / emphasis: compare the scope of B with A: narrower and concrete (example), the same thing made precise (specification), the same thing reworded (restatement), the same direction pushed further (emphasis).
- Sequence and time: follow ordinal cues, dates and durations.

In interrupter items the connective links the whole sentence to the previous one (the subject is the second member of a comparison, so contrast keys dominate: 1.34, 2.6, 2.16). In semicolon items the relation is between the two clauses of one sentence. In 3.2 the blank is immediately followed by "though," so two connectives stack and the key must be compatible with an existing contrast.

Genre: all 75 are single informational paragraphs. There is no literary excerpt with an attribution line, no poem, no paired texts, no notes, no table or graph. Literature items describe a work or its reception (Posey's two genres, two stories in a Lahiri collection, a critic reading an Ashbery poem) and quote at most a title or a short phrase.

How passages are built:

- Two sentences (36 items): S1 states A, S2 opens with the blank and states B. Example 1.36: a law lowers the voting age; ______ more people can take part.
- Three sentences (34 items): S1 context or definition, S2 the claim or first data point (A), S3 the blank and B. Example 1.5: what flying buttresses are; they removed the need for thick walls; ______ walls could be thinner and higher.
- Four sentences (5 easy items): procedures (1.8 turtle nesting, 1.22 pottery) or a longer build-up before the blank (1.27 district sizes, 1.29 a bill resubmitted for years, 1.30 an example sentence followed by an addition).
- The blank sentence is the last sentence in 64 of 75 items (30/16/18). When a sentence follows, it sometimes fixes the relation: 3.1 (Sanmartín "counts herself among neither" shows S2 was only unpacking the debate), 3.19 ("Still, one sees natural influences" shows the blank sentence was a concession), 1.24 (a "for example" sentence after the contrast).
- Other connectives in the passage often expose the structure: "First, ... Finally" around the blank (1.8), "Then," before it (1.35), "For example," just before (1.14, 1.30), "However," just before (1.27) or two sentences earlier (3.9), "but" after the blank (3.17). Easy items carry these cues more often (at least 7 of 37, against 2 of 18 and 1 of 20 by a simple regex count).

Names, dates and places:

- About 41 of 75 passages name a real person (20 easy, 7 medium, 14 hard). First mention gives role plus nationality or heritage ("Ugandan American professor Peter Nazareth", "Tlingit glass artist", "Ivorian artist", "Iraqi artist"); later mentions use the surname. Diacritics are kept (Sanmartín, Bolívar, Domènech i Montaner, Lumière, Martínez).
- 26 passages contain a four-digit year (11/8/7). In time items the dates carry the relation (1891 then 1896 for "Later"; August 1929 then December 1928 for "Earlier").
- Researchers appear as "biologist X", "seismologists X and Y", often with a study year ("A 2022 study by researchers...").
- Places are named precisely (Siletz, Umbria, Laconia, Hverfjall, Toliman, KELT-9).

Vocabulary: educated general register. Share of words with 10 or more letters: 6.5% easy, 9.7% medium, 8.7% hard. Specialist terms are glossed in place (laconic, iconicity, vicariance, cryptocrystalline, cognate replacement), so content knowledge never decides the answer.

## 4. The correct answer

How it is justified:

- The key follows from the relation between A and B. In about 60 items, A is the sentence just before the blank (or the first clause of a semicolon sentence) and nothing else is needed.
- In the rest, the paragraph matters. 3.9 "therefore" draws a conclusion from S2's "However, recent research has demonstrated...". In 1.30, "In addition" adds a second instance after an explicit "For example," sentence. 1.12 "Earlier" depends on "already a record breaker" plus the dates. 3.2, 3.17 and 3.19 depend on a contrast marker after the blank (though, but, Still).
- The key's relation is never shared by a distractor (75 of 75, see 7b). The student is never asked to pick the better of two near-synonyms. The key is the one option that is not false.

How it is worded:

- The key is a stock connective, not a paraphrase of passage content, so there is no echo cue.
- Length gives no cue. Mean words per option: key 1.81 / 1.44 / 1.60 against distractors 1.69 / 1.48 / 1.52 (easy / medium / hard). The key is strictly the longest option in 12 of 75 items and strictly the shortest in 2.
- Answer letters are close to uniform: C is slightly favoured, 22 against 16 to 19 for the other letters.

## 5. Distractor recipes

There are 225 distractor slots. Every distractor is a real, grammatical connective, so each one fails only on logic. Here are the archetypes, named by the false claim the decoy makes about the A-B relation:

1. **False opposition** (contrast or concession word where B continues A): 65 slots, found in 44 of the 54 items whose key is not adversative. Two kinds:
   - Plain contrast (however, by contrast, in contrast, by comparison, in comparison, on the contrary, instead, conversely, on the other hand, rather): 39 slots. Example: in 1.36 the voting age is lowered, so "By contrast" or "Nevertheless" would deny the obvious consequence (more people can vote).
   - Dismissive or persisting concession (nevertheless, regardless, in any case, all the same, admittedly, that said, granted): 26 slots. "Regardless" and "in any case" say A does not matter to B; "nevertheless" says B holds despite A. Example: in 1.6, "Regardless," would cut the link between the purple dye and the name "land of purple".
   - Plus 2 slots where an adversative decoy faces an adversative key (2.4 granted against by contrast; 3.17 however against of course).
2. **False parallel** (similarly, likewise): 27 slots. This is the most common decoy against a contrast key (10 of 45 contrast-key distractors), and it also appears against time (5) and cause (4) keys. It fails when B is the same subject continuing (1.9), an opposite (1.14, 2.6) or a later step (1.35). It is never offered against an addition key.
3. **Unearned cause** (as a result, therefore, thus, consequently, for this reason, hence, accordingly, as such, in turn, plus once to that end and once fittingly): 31 slots, mostly against contrast (9), concession (5), specification (3) and culmination (3) keys. It fails because A does not produce B. Example: in 3.12, "For this reason" would make fusion's promise the cause of its lab failures.
4. **Wrong grain** (example, restatement, specification or emphasis word where B has a different scope): 52 slots (for example/for instance 25, in other words/that is 15, specifically/in particular 7, in fact/indeed/actually 5).
   - "For example" fails when B is not an instance of A but its opposite (1.10, 1.17), its result (1.13) or the next step (1.8).
   - "In other words" fails when B adds new facts rather than rewording A (1.7, 1.24).
   - "Specifically" fails when B is not a narrower version of A (1.10, 1.34).
5. **Additive decoy** (in addition, additionally, moreover, furthermore, plus once alternatively): 20 slots. It fails when B is not a separate item: B may be A's consequence (2.14 "Moreover"), A restated (3.1 "Furthermore") or a concession (3.12, 3.17, 3.19 "Moreover/Furthermore").
6. **Phantom sequence or wrong time** (firstly, secondly, second of all, in the first place, in the second place, lastly, next, finally, meanwhile, today, nowadays, soon, subsequently, previously, earlier, elsewhere): 21 slots.
   - Ordinal decoys ("Firstly", "In the second place", "Second of all", "Lastly") appear only in easy items, where nothing is being listed.
   - Time decoys point the wrong way (1.12 "Next" where the event came first) or invent simultaneity ("Meanwhile" in 1.32, 2.17, 3.5).
7. **Premature summary** (in conclusion, in sum): 7 slots. B is a new particular, not a wrap-up. "In conclusion" appears only in easy items (5 times, never keyed).

Two structural habits:

- **Synonym pair.** In 8 items two distractors share a relation: 1.15 in any case / nevertheless; 1.16 on the other hand / in comparison; 1.22 however / by contrast; 1.25 however / in comparison; 2.5 by comparison / instead; 3.2 accordingly / consequently; 3.5 regardless / admittedly; 3.6 nevertheless / that said. Both members are wrong, so a student who notices they "cannot both be right" can drop them together.
- **Near-family decoy** (same broad family as the key, wrong sub-relation). Only 7 items have one, and each is wrong for a precise reason:
  - 1.4 and 1.26: "in other words" against an example key.
  - 2.15: "for example" against "that is".
  - 1.12: "next" against "earlier" (right family, wrong direction in time).
  - 1.18: "in conclusion" against "later".
  - 2.4: "granted" against "by contrast".
  - 3.17: "however" against "of course".

What separates the tiers:

- **Easy.** The relation shows in the content itself: opposite values (bold wings against colorless wings), dates in order, a procedure with "First ... Finally", a general claim followed by a named instance. Keys come from the basic set. Several decoys are obviously off: In conclusion, Firstly, Second of all, Today, Soon, Elsewhere.
- **Medium.** Same families, with three changes:
  - The blank moves inside the sentence: an interrupter after the subject, or after "In 2014,".
  - Keys widen to hence, accordingly, previously, fittingly, there, in many cases, that is, though.
  - The science gets denser (chiral molecules, trimethylamine N-oxide, chondrules, cognate replacement).

  The relation still lives in two adjacent clauses.
- **Hard.** The relation is rhetorical rather than factual. In hard items the familiar words are the decoys, and the key is the rarer connective (mean bank frequency 4.1 against 8.8). Five recurring hard patterns:
  1. *Concession frames* (6 of 20): a grant before a turn ("Of course" followed by "but" or "Still", 3.17 and 3.19); a qualification of a supported claim ("Granted", 3.8; "That said", 3.12 and 3.14); a claim that holds against an expectation ("Nonetheless", 3.11). In 3.17 "However" is the trap because the sentence's own "but" already carries the contrast.
  2. *Content contrast, logical support*: B's content differs from A's subject, but B supports A's claim. In 3.10, most volcanoes being shallow is exactly why Iceland's are outliers ("indeed"). In 3.6, every working maglev being in Asia reinforces "unrealized in the US" ("In fact"). The adversative decoys (nevertheless, that said) catch students who react to the surface contrast.
  3. *Argument-level links*: "therefore" in 3.9 reaches back two sentences; "to that end" (3.15) and "fittingly" (3.13) require reading intent or aptness, not mechanical cause.
  4. *Restating an abstract claim* (3.1 that is, 3.5 in other words), with addition and contrast decoys.
  5. *Stacked or pre-empted connectives*: in 3.2 ("______ though,") the contrast is already marked, so the key is the non-adversative "ultimately". In 3.7 "Even in this ... work, ______" the key is the adverbial "though".
- **Not a difficulty driver: closeness of options.** Hard items have the fewest same-family decoys (1 of 20). Difficulty comes from reading the relation correctly, not from telling near-synonyms apart.

### 7b. How option sets are built

- **The key's relation is unique in every item (75 of 75).** No distractor shares the key's fine relation.
- **Usually four different relations.** 67 of 75 items (33 easy, 17 medium, 17 hard) use four different fine relations: the three distractors come from three relations that differ from each other and from the key. In the other 8, two distractors share a relation with each other (the synonym pairs in section 5), never with the key.
- **Broad-family view.** 34 items cover four different broad families, 38 cover three, 3 cover two. Most common signatures:
  - adversative + causal + additive + illustrative: 16 items
  - two adversative + illustrative + temporal: 6
  - additive + adversative + causal + temporal: 5
  - two adversative + causal + temporal: 5
  - two adversative + causal + illustrative: 4
- **An adversative option is almost always present.** 65 of 75 items have one (33 easy, 16 medium, 16 hard), and 47 have a causal one.
  - When the key is not adversative (54 items), 44 include at least one adversative decoy.
  - When the key is adversative (21 items), the decoys are continuation words: similarity or addition in 17, example/restatement/specification in 18, causal in 14.
- **Pairings the bank avoids.** These look like deliberate exclusion rules, because the second option would be defensible:
  - Contrast and concession are almost never mixed: a contrast key has a concession decoy once (2.4 granted), and a concession key has a contrast decoy once (3.17 however).
  - Two contrast words appear together only when both are decoys (1.16, 1.22, 1.25, 2.5), never as key plus decoy. The bank never tests however against by contrast, or however against nevertheless.
  - Addition keys never have a similarity decoy; the one similarity key (2.7) has no addition decoy.
  - Example keys never have a specification decoy, and specification keys never have an example decoy. Example against restatement does occur (1.4, 1.26, 2.15).
  - Purpose and aptness keys (with this in mind, to that end, fittingly) never have a cause/effect decoy.
- **Typical easy set:** the key plus three decoys from three families: one adversative (or a causal one when the key is itself adversative), one similarity or addition word, and one "odd" decoy (in conclusion, firstly, in other words, today). Example 1.1, key However: As a result / Similarly / In conclusion.
- **Typical hard set:** a rare key plus three common connectives from three other families. Example 3.19: Furthermore / Similarly / Of course / Thus.

### 7c. Sentence-pair patterns that make each relation correct

**Contrast**
- *Two subjects, one attribute, opposite values, parallel syntax* gives by contrast / in contrast / by comparison (1.14, 1.17, 1.34, 2.4, 2.6, 2.18, 3.18). B usually opens with the second subject and the connective as an interrupter ("A flipped chiral molecule, by contrast, ..."). "By comparison" is used when the attribute is a number (about 180 sunspots against about 2).
- *Established view, then a finding that breaks it* gives However (1.1 the 13,000-year arrival against a 30,000-year date; 2.1 most planets orbit G-type stars, this one a B-type).
- *A positive claim, then a limiting fact about the same subject* gives However (1.10 famous then, neglected now; 1.33 flexible fabric, heavy rhinestones; 1.23 many honors, one ranked above all; 1.24 most species bold, some colorless).
- *Appearance, then expert reality, inside one sentence* gives though (2.16 the volcanoes look alike; specialists can tell them apart). Also *a concession frame continued*: 3.7 "Even in this superficially traditional work, though, ...".

**Concession / qualification**
- *Supported claim, then a fact that weakens it in part* gives Granted / That said (3.8 iconicity predicts learning, but frequency predicts it better; 3.12 fusion's promise, but no net gain before 2022; 3.14 too fine for the eye, but visible under a microscope).
- *Main claim, then an obvious objection the writer concedes, then the claim resumes with "but" or "Still"* gives Of course (3.17, 3.19).
- *Unpromising appearance, then someone finds value anyway* gives Nonetheless (3.11).

**Cause / effect**
- *Condition or change, then its consequence* (1.3, 1.5, 1.15, 1.27, 1.36, 2.9, 2.14).
- *Need or motive, then the same agent's action* (1.9 cost leads to a blended approach; 1.13 a worried mother calls; 1.16 the energy budget leads to gliding; 2.17 impressed voters award the prize: "Accordingly").
- *General principle, then its application* (1.32: every compound has a unique fingerprint; thus the light revealed sodium carbonate).
- *Evidence against a belief, then a verdict on the belief* (3.9 therefore).

**Purpose**: a goal or belief, then a deliberate act serving it (1.21 wanting randomness leads to random techniques; 3.15 a view of Elvis as anthology leads to the course title).

**Appropriateness**: B echoes A aptly (2.5 a poem that inspired a generation of writers, who are then named the Okot School after its author; 3.13 an extreme feat leaves an awestruck audience).

**Addition**: two members of the same list, independent and pointing the same way. Examples: two research methods (2.11), two harms (2.10), two acts by the same person (1.7, 1.30), two themes of one poem (3.20).

**Similarity**: a parallel case, a different member of the same set doing the same thing (2.7: a second story in the collection about a troubled couple).

**Example**: a general claim, often marked by "many", "often", "all kinds of", "two key factors", "collaborates with other artists", then one named instance (1.2, 1.4, 1.20, 1.25, 1.26, 1.28, 1.37). B is clearly narrower and concrete: a person, a work, a term, an anecdote.

**Specification**: a general statement, then the exact content of that same statement (1.19 "hands-on fieldwork", then which digs; 2.12 "investigated the relationship", then which variables; 3.3 "a mix of cultural influences", then which two cultures). "In many cases" (2.3) turns an "overall" verdict into a stronger claim about frequency.

**Restatement**: a technical or abstract phrase, then a plain rewording with no new facts (2.15 defines "cognate replacement"; 3.1 unpacks "divided between dispersalists and vicariancists"; 3.5 rephrases Aristotle's position).

**Emphasis**: a claim, then a stronger fact in the same direction (1.6 famous for purple, then the very name may mean "land of purple"; 3.6 unrealized in the US, then every working line is in Asia; 3.10 extreme outliers, then most volcanoes are shallow).

**Sequence**: an explicit procedure with ordinal cues around the blank (1.8 First / ______ / Finally; 1.22 starts by / ______ / then / At last; 1.11 "first tags", then the software runs).

**Time**:
- Dated or durational events in order: 1.18 Later (1891 to 1896); 1.31 Afterward (failure, then the apology); 1.35 Eventually (after hours or days).
- Reverse order: 1.12 Earlier (December 1928 before August 1929); 2.2 Previously (the assumptions that the 1873 finding overturned).
- End of a long struggle: 1.29 and 2.13 Finally (fifteen years of resubmission; decades of debate).

**Culmination**: a surface fact, then what in the end matters, often next to a contrast cue (3.2 "ultimately, though," the real audience of the letter; 3.4 the mind wanders to planets, then the title brings it back to Earth).

**Alternative**: one method, then a different method for the same problem (3.16 a landfill against phytoremediation).

**Place**: B happens at the place just named (2.8 "There," meaning at the bureau in France).

## 8. Generation blueprint

### Slots of a template

- `domain` and `topic`: weight toward the bank's mix (natural science about 30%, arts and culture about 28%, social science and history about 13% each, literature about 11%).
- `entity`: an invented researcher, artist or place, or a fact that can be checked. Introduce with role plus heritage ("biologist X", "Peruvian sculptor Y"). Do not reuse the bank's people or topics.
- `relation`: one of the 16 fine relations, weighted by tier (table below).
- `A`: the anchor statement. `B`: the blank sentence. Optional `S0` (context before A) and `S+` (a follow-up sentence after B, required for the concession and restatement patterns that the bank resolves after the blank).
- `position`: sentence-initial, interrupter after the subject, after an introductory phrase, after a semicolon, or after an em dash.
- `key`: a connective from the relation's list, picked by tier (core words for easy, rarer ones for hard).
- `d1..d3`: three distractor relations chosen by rule, then one word each.

### Answer logic

The key is right only because of the relation the author built into A and B. The generator must write A and B to express that relation unambiguously, and must make every other relation offered clearly false for that pair. Treat the relation label as data, the way the math side labels each wrong answer with its misconception (`satbank/item.py`): each distractor carries the archetype from section 5 and a one-line reason ("B is not an instance of A").

### Distractor construction (programmatic)

1. Remove the key's own relation, plus any relation the bank treats as defensible next to it:
   - contrast and concession exclude each other;
   - addition and similarity exclude each other;
   - example and specification exclude each other;
   - purpose and appropriateness exclude cause/effect.
2. If the key is not adversative, include one adversative decoy about 80% of the time (44 of 54 in the bank). If the key is adversative, include one similarity or addition decoy and one example, restatement or specification decoy.
3. Fill the remaining slot from causal, temporal or summary decoys. Easy items may use the obvious ones (in conclusion, firstly, second of all, today); hard items should use common connectives (therefore, moreover, similarly, for example) so that the key is the rare word.
4. Allow a synonym pair of decoys in about 10% of items (8 of 75), always from a relation other than the key's.
5. Render each option: capitalize for sentence-initial positions, lowercase otherwise, add a trailing comma, shuffle with a uniform letter distribution.

### What needs an author or a language model

The passage text. A and B must carry the relation through content: opposite values, a date order, a motive and an action, a general claim and a named instance. Hard patterns need careful writing:

- the concession frame with its later "but" or "Still";
- the content-contrast-but-support trap (indeed / in fact);
- an argument-level "therefore";
- the "ultimately" culmination with a following "though".

### Per-subtype recipes

| Subtype | A | B | Key pool (easy to hard) | Typical decoys |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Contrast, two subjects | X has value v on attribute | Y has the opposite value | by contrast, in contrast, by comparison (numbers), conversely (hard, unused as key so far) | similarly, for example, therefore |
| Contrast, belief against finding | long-held view or majority pattern | new evidence or exception | however | as a result, similarly, in conclusion |
| Concession | supported claim | partial limit; or obvious objection followed by but/Still in S+ | that said, granted, of course, nonetheless | for this reason, moreover, specifically |
| Cause / effect | condition, need or principle | consequence, action or application | as a result, for this reason, consequently, thus, hence, accordingly, therefore | nevertheless, by contrast, for example |
| Purpose / aptness | goal or belief; or a notable fact | deliberate act; or a fitting echo | with this in mind, to that end, fittingly | in sum, that is, however |
| Addition / similarity | item 1 of a list; or case 1 | item 2; or a parallel case | in addition, additionally, moreover, what's more; likewise | on the contrary, in other words, thus |
| Example | general claim with a quantifier | one named instance | for example, for instance | however, in conclusion, regardless |
| Specification / restatement / emphasis | general or abstract statement | exact content; rewording; stronger same-direction fact | specifically, that is, in other words, in fact, indeed | in addition, by contrast, nevertheless |
| Sequence / time | step or dated event | next step, later/earlier event, end of a long effort | next, then, later, earlier, previously, afterward, eventually, finally, ultimately | however, similarly, in other words |

Suggested key relation mix per tier (from the bank): easy 22% contrast, 24% cause, 19% example, 22% sequence/time, rest addition/specification/emphasis/purpose, no concession. Medium: contrast and cause lead, plus specification, restatement, addition, aptness, place. Hard: 30% concession, then restatement, emphasis, culmination, purpose/aptness, alternative, and a few contrast with mid-sentence placement.

### Automatic checks

1. Exactly one `______`; four distinct options, each ending with a comma; option case matches the blank position; passage 32 to 66 words in 2 to 4 sentences; blank sentence last in most items.
2. Relation labels: the key's relation appears in no distractor; no excluded pairing (step 1 above); at most one shared relation among the distractors.
3. Grammar: each option inserted gives a well-formed sentence; no subordinators or coordinators in the options; no connective in B that duplicates the key's job (for example "therefore ... thus"), except the deliberate hard patterns where the later contrast marker is required.
4. Blind solve: a model answers without labels and must choose the key. Then a substitution test: for each distractor, the model judges whether the text still reads logically with that word in place. Reject the item if any distractor is judged acceptable, or if the model's justification names a different relation than the label.
5. Originality: low n-gram overlap with the 75 official passages; no reuse of their people, works or topics.

### Quality risks

- **Near-relation ambiguity.** however against nevertheless, therefore against thus against for this reason, in fact against indeed, specifically against for example, in addition against similarly. The bank avoids these pairings entirely; the generator must too.
- **Over-wide decoys.** "However" fits almost any contrast and "for example" fits loose illustration. Never use one as a decoy when the passage has even a partial contrast or instance.
- **Concession items depend on the turn after the blank.** If S+ is dropped or weakened, "however" becomes defensible.
- **Content-contrast traps can tip into real contrast.** The emphasis patterns (indeed / in fact) need a B that plainly supports A's claim.
- **Facts about real people and works.** A generated "true" anecdote can be false. Prefer invented figures with plausible details, or verified, widely documented facts.
- **Register drift.** Keep to the bank's connective list; avoid colloquial ones (anyway, plus, so) and ones that change the grammar (although, because).

### Recommended build

- **Templates.** 14 distinct relation templates:
  - contrast: two-subject, belief-versus-finding, limiting-fact;
  - concession: grant-then-turn, qualification;
  - cause/effect: consequence, motive-action;
  - purpose/aptness;
  - addition;
  - example;
  - specification/restatement;
  - emphasis (content-contrast trap);
  - sequence/time;
  - culmination/alternative.

  Each template has 2 to 3 position variants.
- **Volume.** About 40 easy, 30 medium and 30 hard items at first, keeping the bank's relation mix and a uniform answer-letter spread.
- **Order.** Build first the three subtypes that make up 35 of 75 bank items and 24 of 37 easy items: contrast (two-subject and belief-versus-finding), cause/effect, and example. Then sequence/time (cheap and fully cued). Then the hard concession family, which carries most of the hard tier and needs the most careful writing and checking.
