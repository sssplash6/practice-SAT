# Blind round 2: judge summaries

Caveat: judges recognise most official items from memory (Boundaries 33 of 34, Central Ideas 17 of 17), so AUC over unrecognised items is not meaningful. Use each skill's mean p for our items and the pass count (p 0.5 or below) against round 1, plus the tells.

## boundaries (ours mean 0.65, round 1 0.76; 2 of 34 pass)
1. Fact density: one or two sentences packed with exact counts, years, institution and method (item 3).
2. Template copying: key and distractors copy an official frame (item 62 reuses the Nakalembe "though;" set; items 4 and 10 both use "a distinctive feature: a").
3. Neat abstract opener, then a compressed study summary (item 40).
4. Vocabulary above SAT level (item 46, "instrumental variable").
5. Official cue: plain or slightly clumsy wording, easy topics (item 58).

## central_ideas_and_details (ours mean 0.66, round 1 0.73; 2 of 17 pass)
1. Very dense detail and long noun chains (items 18, 12, 31).
2. Revisionist-scholar template: "A long-standing account holds... Historian X contends the reverse" (items 4, 2, 28).
3. Long options opening with the researcher's name ("Norton argues...") on main-idea items (item 12).
4. Inference puzzles filed as main idea or detail (item 17).
5. Official cues: choppy short sentences, slightly awkward wording, "certain writer", messy translator credits (item 21). Literary imitations were near 0.5.

## command_of_evidence (ours mean 0.78, round 1 0.73; 0 of 33 pass)
1. Quotation stems with unquoted options (T3_person_quote medium and hard): fixed, all options quoted.
2. Too much detail for the skill: parenthetical definitions, named institutions and instruments, "et al." (items 38, 48, 41; item 21 a simple lookup with a long setup).
3. "The table shows" in the passage (items 37, 57). Not a real tell: 5 official items say it.
4. Key that does not support the claim: Q1_P1_lookup.medium (Peru 37 vs 40 did not show the gap). Fixed: Finland 65 overall vs 18 on social media.
5. Keys that need arithmetic or pack a two-part summary with computed differences (Q2_P4_trend.hard, Q2_P8_multipart.hard, Q2_P5_control.medium).
6. Official cues: blunt, slightly clunky easy-tier wording, small native slips.

## cross_text_connections (ours mean about 0.8, round 1 0.84; 0 of 25 pass; all 25 official recognised)
1. Set-up view in Text 1 ("often assume", "the reasoning goes", "Standard accounts"), then one named study in Text 2 that knocks it down (item 17).
2. Too many exact numbers in Text 2 (items 3, 9, 12, 45, 48).
3. Details planted only to feed distractors: a background fact or control case for a distractor to twist (items 46, 36, 42).
4. Hedged keys ("may not", "along with", "accepting X but maintaining Y") with near-miss distractors built from the texts; official distractors are often unrelated or whimsical (item 15 vs official 44).
5. Tier mix: ours all read hard and research-heavy; easy official items have a chatty voice, contractions (item 20).

## form_structure_and_sense (AUC 0.98, round 1 0.71; ours mean 0.70, official 0.22; 3 of 40 pass; nothing recognised, so this is the cleanest measurement)
Worse than round 1: the density push overshot.
1. Passages packed like a research abstract (named researcher with year, institution or reserve, exact numbers, technical or species names) for an easy verb, pronoun or apostrophe item (item 14: 5β-stigmastanol, Sistiaga's team).
2. Discovery or vindication story arcs and vivid anecdotes in flawless prose (items 39, 73).
3. Official cues: loose or awkward wording, drifting topic, typos (items 28 "fewest number", 55, 50).
4. Em dash asides and contracted options appear only in items judged official (item 16).
5. Short, simple passages on general topics with a textbook framing read as official easy items (items 8, 72).

## inferences (round 1 ours 0.76)
1. The passage closes off alternatives in advance: a sentence or control condition added only to make the key airtight ("which rules out the possibility", "during which no tsunami reached the coast", "did not produce metal tools").
2. Famous studies retold neatly with stacked exact figures (Poverty Point, Bernstein's curtains, En-Gedi, Wilmshurst); official items use less-known studies and odder phrasing.
3. Stock distractors: overreach ("more quickly than any other"), a variable the passage never mentions, data restated backward.
4. Stems that point at the key ("Under either interpretation", "Given the work's intended recipients").
5. Official cues: contractions and asides ("But that doesn't mean", "What is intriguing is that"), plain easy-tier style, glosses like "or gene variant".

## words_in_context (round 1 ours 0.75)
1. Very dense evidence: a mechanism, species name or stacked figures packed into a long second sentence, often after a semicolon (items 11, 14, 33, 37).
2. Famous field studies (economics, psychology) retold with three named authors and full method detail (items 31, 32, 45, 19, 26).
3. Set framings: "long regarded as / often dismissed as ... however, X contends", "far from ___", "paradoxically" (items 15, 22, 37, 38).
4. Easy key under a sophisticated, detail-heavy setup (item 20).
5. Distractors built from a passage detail, or filler distractors (items 41, 33, 3).
6. Official cues: loose or casual register ("on first glance", "it's not surprising", "less ___ compared to"), one- or two-sentence setups, public-domain excerpts.

## transitions (round 1 ours 0.87)
1. Too many researched facts: exact counts, named institutions, places, dates (item 48: Jinping, 2,400 m of marble, PandaX; also 9, 51).
2. Long sentences packing several clauses with colons, semicolons and appositives around the blank (items 2, 6).
3. An extra sentence after the key sentence that the logic does not need (items 44, 7, 58).
4. Official templates (Fittingly, Finally, To that end, a Massey-style contrast) reused on a richer topic (items 60, 4, 56).
5. Official cues: plain or slightly clumsy wording, generic "Scientists" or "Researchers", odd filler distractors (Firstly, In conclusion, Lastly).

## rhetorical_synthesis (nothing recognised; round 1 AUC 0.96)
1. Distractors built as traps that distort or overstate the notes ("world's earliest" for "outside Africa", "in Mexico" for "Maya region"); official distractors are mostly accurate and simply miss the goal.
2. Keys that compute a number the notes do not state (142 years, 700,000 years), or long semicolon keys chaining several notes.
3. Polished notes about a recent study ("In 20XX, a team led by X...") with dense science; official notes are plainer, sometimes clunky.
4. Copied official stem templates ("which of the three cities", "define and provide an example", "begin a narrative") and quotation items whose trap is an unquoted paraphrase.
5. Official cues: em dashes in notes or choices, the older "given sentences" stem, small factual slips, nonsense distractors, awkward keys. Easy one-note-per-option items are coin flips.

## text_structure_and_purpose
1. Too much detail packed into a short passage: exact figures, affiliations, glosses, acronyms, often with a closing coda from UNESCO, a reviewer or a "concludes" line (items 51, 30, 78).
2. Passage built backwards from the answer: it has exactly the three-part shape the key describes, and the options differ only in their last clause (items 13, 58, 80).
3. Copied templates from official items in the same set: question then underlined answer, "qualifies", "limitation", two underlined portions, "characterization by two works".
4. A blunt verdict sentence added to anchor an "argue" key (items 10, 42).
5. Official cues: copyright lines, typos and awkward wording, casual register, terse four-option templates. Literary imitations sit near 0.5.
