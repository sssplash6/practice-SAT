The Silk Road is popularly imagined as a highway along which merchant caravans conveyed silk and other luxuries in great volume between China and Rome. But the contracts and other everyday documents preserved in the dry sands of the Taklamakan Desert led historian Valerie Hansen to a far more modest picture: direct commerce between China and Rome was minimal, and most exchange consisted of small-scale transactions among local peddlers. What principally sustained the region’s economy, Hansen contends, was government expenditure, notably the bolts of silk with which the Tang dynasty paid the garrisons stationed on its northwestern frontier.
