For much of the twentieth century, scholars of Hawai‘i relied on a small canon of nineteenth-century Hawaiian writings that had been translated into English. M. Puakea Nogelmeier, a scholar of the Hawaiian language, calls this reliance a “discourse of sufficiency”: the acceptance of a fraction of the sources as adequate to represent the whole. <u>Hawaiian-language newspapers alone, published from 1834 until 1948, filled some 125,000 pages with news, history, literature, and lore, much of which later scholars left untapped.</u> Moreover, Nogelmeier argues, the translations themselves recast their authors’ self-representations to fit a Western framework.
