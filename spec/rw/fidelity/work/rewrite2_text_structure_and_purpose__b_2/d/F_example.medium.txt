Radiocarbon dating of charcoal preserved in the sediments of an ancient channel led archaeologist Ian McNiven and colleagues to conclude that the Gunditjmara people of what is now southeastern Australia were reshaping the waters around the volcano Budj Bim at least 6,600 years ago. Drawing on the abundant volcanic rock of the area’s lava flows, the Gunditjmara <u>built channels, weirs, and dams that controlled the flow of water, enabling them to trap, store, and harvest kooyang, or short-finned eels</u>. UNESCO describes the network as one of the world’s oldest and most extensive aquaculture systems.
